# 6 Months of AI-Assisted Coding Can Make You a Worse Developer

## The Importance of a Strong Engineering Foundation

Every developer starts somewhere. Usually, with the basics of a programming language:

```python
print("Hello world.")
```

We learn A, B, C. Then we start putting them together. Slowly, but steadily, things begin to make sense.

Curiosity kicks in. Learning something new feels exciting. Eventually, we start forming sentences and expressing ourselves through code.

Problems become breakfast. Every day brings a new challenge to solve.

We start committing, then competing... with ourselves. A new hobby unlocked!

Friends could barely get me outside. I'd casually think about how to program a vending machine. Then I'd actually build one. And, of course, move on to something else.

The next logical step? Find a mentor, attend courses, get certified. Crush the first interview, wait six months instead of going to university like normal people, and eventually land the job.

**Congratulations! You made it!**

Welcome to the spaghetti project that nobody understands and barely anybody cares about.

No problem. I've seen worse!

You try to find some logic in the codebase, but you can't. Then you realize you don't have colleagues to guide you through it.

There's only one "person" available. And sooner or later, you'll reach for it.

AI.

You start using it to write code you don't fully understand. Nobody reviews your work or gives you meaningful feedback. So you use AI for that, too.

But there's a problem: you can't build engineering skills by blindly accepting generated solutions.

Six months of supposedly "hands-on" practice later, you might actually be a worse developer than when you started.

Yes, software development isn't just about writing code. That's arguably only 20–30% of the job. But something else has happened: you don't enjoy programming anymore. You're not as confident, skilled, or motivated as you used to be.

I'm not saying AI is the problem. It might be your environment, the lack of mentorship, or simply the way you're using it.

For me, the way back to enjoying programming was teaching.

When I teach, I return to the fundamentals. I focus on understanding concepts instead of constantly diving into the next advanced technology. I have to explain why something works, not just make it work.

And that's a great reminder of something important:

**Being able to generate code isn't the same as knowing how to engineer software.**

Don't let AI take away the curiosity that made you a developer in the first place.
